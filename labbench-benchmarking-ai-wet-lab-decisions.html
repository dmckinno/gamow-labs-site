LabBench: Can AI agents decide what experiment to run next?

This post summarizes our preprint, LabBench: Benchmarking AI wet lab experimental design and decision-making for AI Agents (PDF). Building agents that need to reason about experiments? Work with us.

Biology moves at the speed of its next experiment. Cells grow, differentiate and respond on their own schedules, so every experiment costs weeks of bench time and material. Choosing the wrong one first is the most expensive mistake a lab can make.

Today that choice is made by human experts. Given a set of results, they pick the test that can most efficiently strengthen or falsify the working hypothesis. If AI agents are going to accelerate discovery past human capacity, this is the skill they need, and we need a way to measure it.

That’s why we built LabBench: 20 held-out tasks drawn from the records of real wet labs, each ending in a decision the lab’s scientists actually made.

What LabBench measures

Each task is a snapshot in time. The agent receives the experimental records that existed at a decision point: slide exports, figures, tables, scripts and sequencing outputs. The lab’s own interpretation and what it decided next are withheld. The agent has to write a report that commits to a decision: in six tasks, a ranked set of next experiments; in the rest, which condition, model, comparison or claim the evidence supports.

The tasks span seven experimental domains: nascent transcription, gene regulation, 3D genome organization, assay and library development, target and disease-model validation, hit-to-lead chemistry, and drug-response profiling.

We don’t just check the final answer. Each task is graded against 20–22 binary pass/fail criteria tied to the evidence the agent could see and the hidden lab record behind it. So an agent is scored both on whether it reaches the experts’ decision and on the reasoning that gets it there.

We built the tasks from internal lab records that are unlikely to be recallable from the open internet. We expect the agent to navigate the scientific evidence as a real researcher would, without the task description pointing to the evidence that matters.

The results

We ran five frontier agents, each in its vendor’s own harness, once on every task.

GPT-6 Astra and Claude Opus 5.5 are statistically tied at the top, but Astra got there in a median of 5 minutes per task against Opus’s 35. Behind them sit Grok 4.7, Muse Spark 1.3 and Gemini 3.8 Flash.

Agents fail the same criteria

The most striking pattern is how much the agents agree about what they can’t do. 182 of the 406 criteria, 45%, were passed by no agent at all, and only 31 by all five. Pairs of agents agree on 70–84% of verdicts, well above what their pass rates alone would predict. What one frontier agent misses, the others usually miss too.

They identify and qualify; they rarely choose

The criteria most agents pass ask for single, local steps the records make explicit: saying what a measurement is (a modeled cluster’s size is not a cell frequency), declining an overclaim (a predicted drug response is not a completed validation), or reconstructing what an analysis did.

The criteria they miss ask them to commit. Criteria that require choosing, committing or ranking pass 21% of the time, against 47% for identifying what something is. Nine of the 23 core decisions were never passed. And in the six tasks that ask for ranked next experiments, no agent passed any of the 13 criteria on which experiment should come first.

Where agents differ

The two leaders have different strengths. GPT-6 Astra leads on core decisions (52%) and on saying what a measurement means (56%); Claude Opus 5.5 leads on integrating evidence (53%), confounds and scope (52%). Gemini 3.8 Flash trails most on evidence integration and evidential status (11–12%). But one row is nearly empty for everyone: of the 70 experiment-design criteria, the best agent passes 9%.

Across the benchmark, analysis tasks are the most tractable. Review and design tasks, where the agent has to judge a finished piece of work or commit to a plan, score 23–24% on average, and assay development, target validation and hit-to-lead chemistry (25–28%) are the hardest themes.

The knowledge is there. The judgment isn’t.

A benchmark full of never-passed criteria invites an obvious critique: maybe the tasks are unfair, and no agent could reasonably get there. We tested that directly.

We took five core decisions that no agent had passed, from five different themes. For each, we appended a single sentence to the brief that points at evidence the agent already had, without stating the answer or adding any domain knowledge. Then we reran GPT-6 Astra, which had failed all five in the benchmark.

With one sentence, it passed all five.

Here is what those sentences did in three of the cases.

Drug response

The evidence
A death-inducing compound triggers a stronger early NF-κB signature than three other death inducers, and an inhibitor that rescues the cells also lowers it. No experiment tests whether the response actually drives death.

What agents did
Four of five designed the right experiment, a selective NF-κB perturbation measuring both the response and death, and then ranked it second.

The hint
“Rank first the experiment that could tell a cause from a bystander.” The decisive experiment moved to first, and the task score rose from 4/20 to 15/20.

Assay development

The evidence
Two tagmentation traces: one peaks at 210 bp with a declining tail; the other peaks at 238 bp but carries a broad shoulder of large, incompletely cut fragments.

What agents did
Every agent chose the larger 238 bp peak and overlooked the shoulder.

The hint
“Judge each tagmentation trace by its whole size distribution, not only by where its peak falls.” The agent chose the lab’s condition.

Gene regulation

The evidence
A cell type’s support is counted as pairwise wins between datasets.

What agents did
No agent noticed that one unusually high dataset can supply many of those wins on its own.

The hint
“Check whether the evidence for a cell type could come mostly from a single dataset.” The agent explained exactly that, and the task score rose from 7/20 to 12/20.

None of these hints teaches the agent any biology. They redirect attention to evidence it already had. We read this as a gap in reasoning rather than in knowledge: the agents hold the biological knowledge these decisions require, but don’t bring it to bear unprompted.

Where experimental design breaks down

To run experiments autonomously, agents need a working model of what experiments cost and of the specific evidence that would count against a hypothesis. Two tasks show how far there is to go.

Choosing the discriminating experiment

A new assay for labeling nascent RNA joins a chemical handle to RNA ends, and synthetic-oligo tests had shown that the join forms. Two untested steps could still break the assay, so the lab’s next two experiments isolated them cheaply: whether reverse transcriptase can copy across the chemical join, and whether the join forms in cell lysate. Four of five agents instead ranked first an experiment on a step the lab hadn’t flagged, and no agent placed the lysate test in its top two.

Weighing cost

A lab had already started a small pilot comparing a disease-variant cell line with its unedited parent, and planned a broad compound screen in the same pair only once the drug-free genotype signature proved repeatable. No agent committed to this cheap, gated path. Claude Opus 5.5 opened with a new differentiation into monocytes and macrophages and pushed the screen to a third stage; GPT-6 Astra began with a single-compound dose-response and then moved to 24-day differentiated cells. Both spend weeks before establishing that the signal exists.

Why this matters

Frontier agents already know a lot of biology, enough to work alongside expert biologists when experimental design stays with humans. But in real experimentation, wall-clock time is irreducible, which makes autonomous and correct experimental design arguably the single most important capability for making AI agents superhuman at this work.

LabBench measures exactly that and penalizes failures along this axis. Better results on LabBench should mean agents that can carry out supervised, autonomous wet-lab work, and eventually run full programs themselves. We’ll keep measuring as the frontier moves.
